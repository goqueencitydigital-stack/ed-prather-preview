Buying New Construction in Denver: A Due-Diligence Guide

Use a practical question list to evaluate a Denver new-build home, compare documents, plan inspections and understand contract decisions before committing.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·Updated November 9, 2026·10 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

Buying new construction in Denver still calls for careful review of the property, contract, financing, inspections and closing details. Before committing, clarify what is included, what remains to be selected, how construction progress will be documented and which dates or conditions appear in the signed contract. Arrange an independent inspection strategy, review available association materials and compare your lender’s estimates. A new home is not a substitute for due diligence: make decisions from the actual documents, property and written answers. Colorado Division of Real Estate home-buying guide.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • Ask for written, property-specific answers about inclusions, selections, plans, warranties and construction milestones rather than relying on general explanations.
  • Use the signed contract to track deadlines and conditions, and arrange inspections suited to the home’s stage and components.
  • Compare loan and closing estimates, review any association documents, and confirm what must be completed or delivered before possession.

Begin with the exact home and the exact offer

New construction can involve choices that are not present in the same form when buying an existing home: a lot, a plan, a stage of completion, finish selections and work still to be done. Treat each as a question to resolve in writing. Ask which home and lot the proposed agreement covers, what plans and specifications identify that home, and whether any features shown in marketing materials are excluded or optional. Do not assume a model home, sample finish or verbal description establishes what will be delivered.

Use the Colorado new-construction guide to organize questions about specifications, inspections and handoff. If architectural style is also shaping your shortlist, read the modern-home guide separately: modern describes a design direction, while new construction describes the building’s stage or age.

Build a comparison file for each property you are considering. Include the address or lot identifier, plan version, included features, selected upgrades, remaining choices, estimated completion information provided to you, and the person responsible for answering questions. Keep versions of plans and written selections together. If an answer changes your decision, ask how it will appear in the contract or another signed document. This is practical recordkeeping, not a substitute for reading the agreement.

Colorado’s Division of Real Estate emphasizes that a sales contract is legally binding and contains transaction deadlines; buyers should understand its terms and seek legal advice when needed. Review the actual contract rather than assuming a builder’s process or a general checklist overrides it. Colorado’s home-buying guidance is a useful starting point. For help organizing purchase questions, compare this process with the broader home-buying resources.

Make a written scope and selection checklist

Before evaluating price or timing, separate what is confirmed from what remains uncertain. Request a list of included items and identify the documents that control each choice. Ask how substitutions, unavailable selections or changes are recorded, who must approve them, and how you will receive an updated written record. These are questions for the seller or builder; the answers depend on the particular transaction and documents.

Use the table as a discussion tool, not as a promise about what a builder must provide. If a feature matters to accessibility, daily use, storage or future maintenance, state why it matters and ask for a precise written answer. Avoid treating a rendering or a broad phrase such as upgraded as a full specification.

Read the contract as a calendar and decision map

Identify every date and condition in the signed agreement, then place them in one calendar. For each item, note what must happen, who acts, what documentation is needed, and what the contract says if the condition is not met. The Colorado Division of Real Estate explains that contracts can contain financing, appraisal, inspection, title, survey, covenant, HOA-document and existing-home-sale conditions. The contract language determines the process and consequences; a condition should not be assumed to work the same way in every agreement. Review the state’s sales-contract overview.

Ask how the schedule relates to construction progress. If the home is not complete when you sign, ask which milestone controls a later decision, what information you will receive, and whether any dates can change under the written agreement. Do not assume a projected completion date is a guaranteed possession date. Ask your broker or attorney to explain language you do not understand, especially language concerning deposits, default, termination, changes and dispute steps.

Earnest money is a good-faith deposit, but its amount and refund conditions depend on the signed contract; it is generally held by a title company in Colorado. A return is not automatic just because plans change. Review the deposit amount, delivery instructions, deadlines and contractual conditions before paying, and keep proof of delivery. The state buying guide explains earnest money and contract deadlines.

Plan inspections around access and construction stage

Ask what inspection access is available, how appointments are arranged, and whether the home can be inspected at more than one stage. Then choose an inspection plan based on the actual home and the questions you need answered. The state describes a home inspection as a review of general condition and major components, not an exhaustive determination of every possible issue. It notes that qualified specialists may be appropriate for concerns such as sewer or structural questions. Read the state’s inspection guidance and make sure you understand any limits of the inspection you arrange.

Request clear access instructions and ask what areas or systems will be unavailable or unfinished during a visit. If an inspector identifies an issue outside their expertise, ask what specialist could assess it and whether access can be arranged. Keep inspection observations separate from your own conclusions: record the location, what was observed, the inspector’s recommendation and any follow-up. If you intend to raise an issue under the contract, follow its written procedure and dates rather than relying on an informal conversation.

Do not equate new with inspected or defect-free. A new-build inspection is a chance to ask informed questions about the particular property and document what you observe. It cannot guarantee future performance. Ask how reported items will be documented, who will respond, and how you can verify the status before closing or possession.

Check financing, appraisal and cash-to-close assumptions

Ask your lender how the loan process will line up with the purchase contract and the anticipated construction schedule. Confirm which property details or documents the lender still needs, whether your current Loan Estimate reflects the transaction as understood, and how updates will be communicated if the closing date or property information changes. Do not use an informal estimate as a substitute for the lender’s current disclosures.

An appraisal is a professional opinion of value by a licensed appraiser; it is usually selected by the lender and paid for by the buyer unless the contract says otherwise. The state advises buyers to understand the agreement’s appraisal terms. See the Colorado buying-process guide. Ask what your contract says if the appraised value differs from the purchase price and what choices or deadlines apply. Avoid assuming the lender will cover a difference or that a particular outcome is automatic.

Compare Loan Estimates of the same type and pay attention to origination charges, required services and lender credits. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that points exchange upfront payment for a lower rate, while lender credits can exchange a higher rate for lower upfront costs. Estimated cash to close combines down payment and closing costs with deposits, credits and adjustments. Taxes and insurance may be escrowed or paid separately, so confirm the assumptions with the lender. Use the CFPB Loan Estimate explainer rather than relying on a single headline payment.

Review association and property responsibilities

Find out whether the home is in an association and, if so, request the documents described in the Colorado residential contract. The state encourages buyers to review governing and financial documents, assessments, meeting minutes, association insurance, management and maintenance responsibilities. A low regular assessment alone does not establish an association’s financial health. Use Colorado’s HOA buying guidance to shape your review.

Ask which items the association maintains and which remain the owner’s responsibility. Do not assume new construction means an association has no maintenance issues, or that every home in a development has the same insurance arrangement. Read the actual documents and identify upcoming or special assessments, rules relevant to your planned use, and any unanswered questions. If documents arrive late in your process, compare their review period and your options with the signed contract; do not infer rights from a document title alone.

Insurance needs also depend on the property and the actual association documents. Ask an insurance professional what information is needed to evaluate the home and any association coverage. For broader planning and document organization, the buyer consultation page can help you prepare questions for your transaction team.

Prepare a pre-possession verification routine

Before closing or possession, compare the home with your written selections, plans and open-item record. Ask how you can review completed work, receive operating information and identify any item still unresolved. Keep the process specific: list each location or feature, the evidence you expect to see, and who will respond. Do not rely on memory or a casual walk-through conversation to capture a material concern.

  1. Gather the signed contract, amendments, plans, selections, inspection reports and current written status of open items.
  2. Walk through the property methodically, matching visible features to the documents and noting questions by room or system.
  3. Ask for clarification where the home, written selection and explanation do not align; record the response and any promised follow-up.
  4. Review the contract calendar and confirm with your transaction team which documents and steps remain before closing or possession.
  5. Keep copies of all final records in a home file for later reference.

A final walk-through is an opportunity to observe the home and raise questions, not a substitute for the inspection or contract process. The goal is to know what you are accepting, what remains documented and what the agreement says about unresolved matters. A careful file also makes future maintenance and insurance conversations easier.

Use a decision log instead of relying on memory

New construction can create a steady stream of small decisions. A simple decision log reduces confusion: date, question, answer, source document, person who replied, and next action. Mark each item as confirmed, pending or not applicable. When a decision affects cost, timing, scope or your willingness to proceed, ask whether the signed documents need to be updated. If they do, retain the final executed version with the rest of the file.

At each review point, ask yourself: Do I understand the contract conditions and dates? Is the home’s scope documented? Have I planned inspections based on access and unanswered questions? Do financing and cash-to-close estimates reflect the current transaction? Have I reviewed the association materials, if applicable? Are the remaining decisions written down and assigned? These questions help you notice gaps without assuming a particular builder practice or outcome.

For support coordinating a purchase alongside a sale or other move, explore sell-and-buy planning. A deliberate process lets you compare the actual documents and property rather than making a decision based only on the appeal of a new home.

Frequently asked questions

Should I get an independent inspection on a new construction home?+

Consider arranging an inspection suited to the home’s actual stage, access and components. Colorado describes a home inspection as a review of general condition and major components, and notes that qualified specialists may be appropriate for certain concerns. Ask what the inspection will and will not cover, and how access will work. If an issue falls outside the inspector’s expertise, ask what specialist could evaluate it and whether the contract’s procedures or dates affect your next step.

Is earnest money automatically refundable if construction or plans change?+

No general refund should be assumed. Colorado’s buying guidance says earnest money is a good-faith deposit and that its amount and refund conditions depend on the signed sales contract. Review the deposit terms, deadlines and conditions in your actual agreement, and keep proof of payment. If a change affects your willingness to proceed, check the written contract and get transaction-specific guidance before relying on a verbal explanation or assuming the deposit will be returned.

What should I compare in a new-construction Loan Estimate?+

Compare Loan Estimates that are for the same kind of transaction, focusing on total origination charges, required services and lender credits. The CFPB explains that points trade upfront cost for a lower rate, while lender credits can reduce upfront costs in exchange for a higher rate. Also confirm how deposits, credits, adjustments, taxes and insurance are reflected in estimated cash to close, and ask the lender whether the estimate matches the current property and transaction details.
