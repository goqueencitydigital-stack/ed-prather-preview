Denver Final Walk-Through Checklist: Prepare for Possession

Use this Denver final walk-through checklist to compare the home with your agreement, document open questions and plan a clear handoff before possession.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·10 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

A final walk-through is a practical opportunity to observe the home, compare what you see with the information and agreements available to you, and raise questions through the appropriate transaction contacts. It does not, by itself, create a new contractual right or replace the signed contract. Bring a room-by-room checklist, take clear notes, and contact your broker or other qualified professional promptly if an observation raises a concern. Let the written agreement and its deadlines guide next steps. Colorado Division of Real Estate home-buying guide.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • Use the visit to record observable conditions and questions, not to assume an outcome or a new right.
  • Compare observations with the written agreement and prior documentation available to you.
  • Route concerns promptly through your broker, attorney or other appropriate professional.

Know what the walk-through is for

Approach the final walk-through as a focused observation visit. Your task is to notice and document what you can see, identify questions and compare those observations with the written information and agreements available to you. Keep that purpose distinct from deciding what a contract means or what remedy might apply.

The Colorado Division of Real Estate explains that a sales contract is legally binding and contains transaction deadlines. It advises buyers to understand the terms and seek an attorney when needed (Colorado home-buying process guidance). Use the signed agreement to determine any rights or obligations related to the walk-through. Do not assume the visit itself changes the contract. If an observation raises a concern, promptly ask your broker or attorney how to handle it under the signed agreement.

Before scheduling or attending, confirm the arranged access and the purpose of the visit with your transaction contacts. Gather the contract and any relevant written agreements or records you already have. Do not assume that a prior conversation, a listing description or a remembered expectation has the same meaning as language in the signed documents.

Think of the visit as a way to reduce uncertainty, not as a guarantee that every condition can be discovered. Some questions may require records, professional expertise or a written response from someone else. Your checklist should help you record those questions rather than encourage you to make technical or legal conclusions on the spot.

Prepare a compact field worksheet

Use a paper or digital worksheet with one line for each room or area. Keep notes factual: what you observed, where, when and whether you have a photo. Add a separate column for the document or person you need to consult. This format makes it easier to communicate a precise question afterward.

Use neutral language, such as “water visible below the sink at 2:15 p.m.” instead of “the plumbing is defective.” The first records an observation; the second makes a technical conclusion you may not be qualified to make. Photos can help explain a location, but keep the written description because an image may not show timing, context or what you actually noticed.

Bring the right documents and expectations

Review the signed contract and any relevant written amendments or agreements before the visit. Make a short list of items you want to compare, such as included property or an agreed repair, without assuming that every expectation is written into the contract. If you cannot identify the applicable document, ask your broker or attorney rather than guessing.

The state publishes current real estate forms, including an Inspection Resolution and a Post-Closing Occupancy Agreement, and lists versions with different effective dates. The form names alone do not establish what applies to a particular transaction or what rights a party has (Colorado Division of Real Estate contracts and forms). Use the appropriate version and ask a qualified professional to interpret the actual paperwork.

Bring a charged phone or camera, a flashlight if useful, a pen and your worksheet. If another person is accompanying you, agree on one note-taking method so observations do not become contradictory. Avoid dismantling equipment, moving heavy items or attempting repairs during the visit. If something is inaccessible, record that fact and ask how it can be addressed.

It may help to review earlier inspection notes, agreed communications and relevant property information. However, the walk-through is not a substitute for a home inspection or specialist review. The state’s home-buying guide describes a home inspection as limited to general condition and major components and notes that qualified specialists may be needed for issues such as sewer scopes or structural concerns (Colorado home-buying process guidance).

Move through the property in a repeatable order

Begin at the entrance and proceed through the property in the same sequence each time: exterior or shared access where included, entry, main living areas, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, utility areas and any included storage. Adapt the route to the actual property. A consistent order reduces the chance of skipping a room when you are focused on a visible issue.

In each room, pause long enough to observe the overall condition, then check the items relevant to your written list. Note visible damage, changes, missing items or belongings left behind. Do not try to infer when a condition began or who caused it unless you have reliable information. A clear location and description are more useful than speculation.

For appliances and systems, record what is visible and what you were able to observe. Do not treat a quick visual check as a technical inspection, and do not assume that a system’s appearance establishes its performance. If an item appears different from an agreed description or raises a safety question, document it and seek appropriate guidance instead of experimenting with unfamiliar controls.

Outside, look at visible access areas, attached spaces and any area included in your transaction documents. Note conditions without extending the visit into an evaluation of every boundary, structure or hidden component. If an issue requires measurements, records or expertise, write down what is needed and who may be able to provide it.

Separate observation from interpretation

Use a two-part note: first, the observable fact; second, the question it raises. For example, record that a cabinet is empty or that an item is not visible, then ask whether a written agreement addresses it. This keeps your message useful and avoids turning an uncertain observation into a conclusion about responsibility.

Compare what you observe with the contract and written agreements, not only with memory or informal descriptions. Colorado contract guidance identifies possible conditions involving financing, appraisal, inspection, title, covenants and association documents, and explains that contract language should specify what must happen, deadlines and what may follow if conditions are unmet or waived (Colorado sales-contract guidance). Do not assume a walk-through observation changes those terms.

If you find a difference, capture the location and context, then contact the appropriate transaction professional. Ask what document governs the question, whether any response is needed before a deadline, and who should communicate with the other party. Let that professional advise you about the process; do not infer that an informal conversation changes the signed agreement.

A calm, specific report is more effective than a long list of conclusions. Group observations by room, distinguish urgent questions from routine ones and attach only relevant photographs. Keep a copy of what you sent and note when and how you sent it.

Use a focused checklist for common areas

For the kitchen, note the visible condition of counters, cabinets, sink area, included appliances and any item named in a written agreement. For bathrooms, note visible conditions around fixtures and surfaces, along with any apparent change from information you have. These are observation prompts, not tests of hidden plumbing or a warranty of performance.

In living spaces and bedrooms, look for visible damage, personal property remaining, changes to walls or floors, and items you expect to be present based on the documents. In storage and utility areas, note what you can safely see and whether access is available. Avoid handling unfamiliar equipment or treating an inaccessible area as proof of a specific condition.

At the entry and outside, record keys or access items presented, the condition of visible doors and paths, and any included area that appears different from your records. If there is an association or shared area, limit your notes to what is relevant to the unit and the agreed access; do not assume a brief visit tells you how maintenance is allocated.

Use this checklist as a prompt, not a scorecard. A box checked does not certify that an item meets a legal, technical or contractual standard. The goal is an accurate record and an organized follow-up question where one is needed.

Handle concerns through the proper channel

If an observation seems important, report it promptly to your broker or attorney and explain what you saw, when you saw it and where. Ask whether the signed documents set a relevant deadline or specify a process. A broker can help direct transaction questions within their role; seek legal advice from an attorney when you need interpretation or legal guidance.

Do not assume that the earnest money will automatically be returned or that a particular observation automatically permits a particular outcome. The state explains that earnest money amount and refund conditions depend on the signed sales contract and that return is not automatic or universal (Colorado home-buying process guidance; sales-contract guidance). Ask a qualified professional to apply the actual contract to your situation.

If a condition appears to need specialist attention, ask who should assess it and whether access can be arranged. Do not undertake a repair or make a promise on behalf of another party. Save written communications, photos and any response together so that your next decision is based on a complete record.

When the issue appears minor or uncertain, it is still reasonable to document it and ask a concise question. The point is not to escalate every difference; it is to avoid losing important information while you are preparing for possession.

Finish with a clear handoff

Before leaving, scan your worksheet for blank rooms, unanswered questions and photos that need labels. Make a short summary with three headings: observed, needs clarification and follow-up contact. Separate items already resolved from questions still waiting for a response.

Confirm the practical handoff details through the people handling the transaction. Keep access instructions, possession information and any agreed occupancy arrangements tied to the written documents and current directions. The state’s official form library includes a Post-Closing Occupancy Agreement, but the form’s existence does not establish permission or terms in an individual transaction (Colorado real estate forms library).

Use your notes to plan the first hours in the home: which items need attention, what can wait and whom to contact if a question remains. Keep transaction papers and key contacts accessible rather than packing them away. If a new concern appears after the visit, record it separately and seek guidance rather than assuming the walk-through process has already answered it.

A careful final walk-through is organized, factual and bounded. Observe what is visible, compare it with the written materials, document uncertainty and let the signed agreement and qualified professionals guide any next step.

For help organizing your purchase questions, review buying a home or arrange a consultation focused on your transaction. Bring the documents and observations that prompted your questions so the conversation can stay specific.

Frequently asked questions

Does a final walk-through create a new right to cancel or renegotiate?+

Do not assume it does. The walk-through is a practical opportunity to observe and document the home, but the signed agreement and applicable transaction documents control the parties’ rights and deadlines. Colorado’s Division of Real Estate describes sales contracts as legally binding and advises buyers to understand their terms and seek an attorney when needed. If you notice a concern, report the facts promptly and ask your broker or attorney how the written agreement applies to your situation.

What should I do if I notice a problem during the visit?+

Write down what you can observe, including the room or location, date and a plain description. Take a relevant photo if it helps communicate the observation, then contact your broker or attorney promptly and ask which written document or deadline may apply. Avoid diagnosing the cause or assuming a particular remedy. If the issue appears technical, ask whether a qualified specialist should assess it and how access can be arranged.

Should the final walk-through replace an inspection?+

No. Treat the walk-through as a separate, brief observation visit rather than a substitute for inspection work. The Colorado Division of Real Estate says a home inspection is limited to general condition and major components and notes that qualified specialists may be needed for matters such as sewer scopes or structural concerns. If a technical question remains, consult the appropriate professional and follow your transaction contacts’ guidance about next steps.
