Buying a Condo in Denver: Look Beyond the Unit

Buying a Denver condo? Review the association, building condition, financial documents, insurance and daily-use rules before you decide on a property.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·Updated November 9, 2026·10 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

When buying a condo in Denver, evaluate the home and the association as connected parts of one decision. Look beyond finishes to the governing and financial documents, assessments, meeting minutes, shared-area responsibilities, association insurance and maintenance arrangements. Verify what the actual records say about the unit and common elements instead of assuming all condos allocate costs or coverage alike. Pair that review with your inspection, financing and closing plans, and resolve material questions within the process and deadlines in your signed contract. Colorado Division of Real Estate HOA buying guidance.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • Assess the unit's condition alongside the association's financial, governance, insurance and maintenance documents.
  • Verify the actual boundaries between the unit, shared elements and owner responsibilities rather than relying on condo assumptions.
  • Connect unresolved document or property questions to the conditions and deadlines in your signed transaction contract.

A condo can look complete during a showing while important ownership questions sit in the association records. The unit's layout and condition matter, but so do shared areas, maintenance allocations, insurance arrangements, association finances and the documents that govern use. Those details can vary, so neither a building's label nor a seller's shorthand should substitute for checking the actual records.

The Colorado Division of Real Estate says buyers under the residential contract are entitled to association documents described in Section 7. It recommends reviewing governing and financial documents, regular and special assessments, meeting minutes, association insurance, management, maintenance and common-element responsibilities. The same guidance cautions that a low regular assessment does not establish financial health. Read the state HOA buying guidance alongside the documents for the particular property.

This article focuses on condo-specific decision making, not a generic home search. For related comparisons, see Denver townhome versus condo ownership questions and the HOA document review checklist.

Separate the unit from the building and association

Start by drawing three circles on your notes: the space you will occupy, the components or areas shared with others, and the association or management structure. Use the governing documents and any available property records to understand the boundaries and responsibilities. Do not assume a wall, window, balcony, roof, plumbing line or other feature belongs to or is maintained by a particular party based solely on its location or common condo terminology.

The broader Colorado condominium buying guide can help you compare day-to-day fit with the ownership and document questions in this Denver checklist. Keep both beside the actual association records rather than assuming every condominium carries the same responsibilities.

For every feature that would affect your decision, write down the document that describes it and the question it raises. For example: “The listing refers to a private patio; which document defines its status and maintenance?” This does not presume what the answer will be. It directs attention to the recorded and association materials relevant to the specific unit.

Make the same distinction for the shared spaces you expect to use. The association documents may describe common elements and maintenance responsibilities, but specific allocations must be verified. If a feature is particularly important, such as storage, parking, access or an outdoor area, seek a clear answer about its status, use and responsibility before treating it as part of the purchase you are making.

Review association records as part of due diligence

Ask your broker to obtain the association documents from the seller, as the Colorado Division of Real Estate HOA guidance recommends. Create an inventory: governing documents, financial records, regular and special assessments, minutes, insurance information, management details and descriptions of maintenance and common-element responsibilities. Record when each document arrived and what time period it covers. If something appears missing, ask rather than assuming there is no relevant record.

Read documents in layers. First, identify rules and responsibilities that affect your intended use. Next, review association finances and assessment information. Then use minutes and management materials to understand issues the association has discussed. Keep three labels in your notes: confirmed by document, explained but not verified, and still open. This prevents a verbal summary or a short excerpt from becoming the basis for a larger conclusion.

The signed contract matters to this review. Colorado's Division of Real Estate explains that contracts can include HOA-document conditions and that contract terms should specify deadlines and what happens if a condition is unmet or waived. Do not assume that the time you need for review is automatically available. Check the contract and ask about its specific process promptly. See the sales-contract guidance.

Compare the assessment with the financial picture

Record the regular assessment, what the documents say it covers, and any special assessments described. Then examine the financial information provided and connect it to the minutes and stated maintenance needs. The monthly amount is only one input. It does not, by itself, tell you whether planned work is funded, whether expenses are changing, or whether there are unresolved concerns. Avoid treating a single figure as a complete measure of association finances.

Separate past costs, current obligations and possible future questions. Minutes may contain discussion without establishing a final action. Look for later records or ask for clarification. If the packet does not answer an important question, document that uncertainty and consider how it affects your budget and comfort with the purchase rather than making up a forecast.

Investigate maintenance and building condition

A unit inspection and association review answer different kinds of questions. An inspection can help you understand the condition of the areas and components within its scope. The Colorado Division of Real Estate describes a home inspection as a general-condition review of major components and notes that specialists may be needed for concerns such as structural issues or a sewer scope. Do not assume a standard inspection covers every shared area or system. See the state home-buying guide.

Ask the inspector what is within the inspection's scope and what could not be evaluated. If a concern involves a shared system, building component or area beyond the unit, use the association records and appropriate specialist input to understand the next question. A finding is not a complete diagnosis of an entire building, and an association document is not a substitute for inspection of the unit.

Compare observed condition with maintenance responsibility. If the unit has a concern that might involve a shared component, identify who is expected to investigate and what the records say about responsibility. Ask whether the association has relevant maintenance information, meeting discussions or management contacts. Do not assume an association will handle a repair merely because the issue touches a common area; verify what the documents provide.

Understand insurance from actual documents

Condo buyers should investigate both the association's insurance materials and their own insurance needs. Do not presume that the association policy covers the unit's interior, personal property, improvements, liability, or every shared component. Conversely, do not assume an individual owner is responsible for every building feature. The Colorado Division of Real Estate HOA guidance says to determine association and individual insurance needs from the actual documents.

Read the policy or coverage materials provided and note the covered property, exclusions, conditions, limits or other details that prompt questions. The Colorado Division of Insurance says summaries explain major coverages and exclusions but do not replace the policy. Ask an insurer about the specific property and how your individual policy should be considered. The Colorado Division of Insurance homeowners guidance offers general resources, not a determination of coverage for this condo.

Make a two-column list: association document says, and question for my insurer. Include any uncertainty about shared versus individual responsibility. Do not infer quote availability, premium, underwriting approval or exact policy terms from the association packet or a general explanation.

Test everyday fit, not just the floor plan

Imagine ordinary routines in the specific unit and building. Where will deliveries be received? How are shared entrances or amenities accessed? What do the documents say about the areas you plan to use? What maintenance tasks might require coordination? These questions are not a substitute for a showing or a rule review; they help you connect the written materials to day-to-day ownership.

Build a “must confirm” list for features that affect your decision: parking or storage descriptions, access arrangements, planned personal use, possible alterations, and any rules you need to understand. Verify each item against the listing, purchase documents, association records or other relevant source. If a feature is described differently across materials, record the inconsistency and ask for an explanation before relying on it.

Consider noise, natural light, layout, storage and work-from-home needs by evaluating the actual unit and your own use. Avoid turning one visit into a conclusion about every time of day or future condition. A second visit, targeted question or specialist review may be useful where the answer matters to your decision.

Connect condo findings to contract decisions

Make a concise summary of issues by category: unit condition, shared responsibility, association finances, insurance, use, and missing information. Then mark each item as resolved, understood but uncertain, or unresolved. This helps you distinguish an issue that needs a practical answer from one that may affect a contractual decision.

Colorado contract guidance notes that inspection, title, HOA-document and other conditions may be included in a contract. It also explains that terms should specify what must happen, deadlines, rights if unmet or waived, and termination provisions. Do not assume that an inspection concern, unanswered HOA question, or title issue automatically gives a buyer a particular remedy. Use the signed contract and get advice suited to the question.

For concerns about title documents, note what the title commitment identifies as requirements and exceptions. The Colorado Division of Insurance explains that a title commitment identifies the insurer, premiums, requirements before policy issuance and exceptions such as covenants and easements. Owner and lender policies protect different interests, and coverage depends on the actual policy and exceptions. Review the state title insurance guidance and the commitment for this transaction.

Use a condo buyer's review checklist

  1. Verify which areas and features are included with the unit and how the documents describe them.
  2. Obtain and inventory the association documents identified for the transaction.
  3. Review governing rules for your intended use and record unresolved interpretations.
  4. Compare assessments, financial information, minutes and stated maintenance needs.
  5. Identify unit and shared-component concerns that need inspection or specialist follow-up.
  6. Read association insurance information and ask an insurer about individual coverage questions.
  7. Check the signed contract for applicable inspection, HOA and title conditions and deadlines.
  8. Decide whether each unresolved item is acceptable, needs clarification or changes your decision.

A condo decision is strongest when it accounts for the specific unit and the association structure together. Keep source documents with your notes and avoid substituting assumptions for verification. To compare another ownership arrangement, use the townhome-versus-condo guide or browse homebuying resources.

Frequently asked questions

What is most important to review when buying a Denver condo?+

Review the association's governing and financial documents, regular and special assessments, minutes, insurance, management, maintenance and common-element responsibilities, as the Colorado Division of Real Estate recommends. Also evaluate the unit's condition and verify how the actual documents define the unit and shared areas. The right priorities depend on your intended use and budget. Track missing records and connect material questions to the deadlines and conditions in your signed contract.

Does a condo association maintain every exterior feature?+

Do not assume that every exterior feature is the association's responsibility. Maintenance and common-element allocations vary, and the Colorado Division of Real Estate advises buyers to review the actual documents to determine association and individual responsibilities. Check the governing and maintenance records for the particular building and ask about unclear language. A broad label such as “exterior included” is not a substitute for verifying what applies to the unit and the feature in question.

Does the association's insurance replace an individual condo policy?+

Do not assume that it does. The Colorado Division of Real Estate advises determining association and individual insurance needs from the actual documents. Review the association insurance materials and ask an insurer how individual coverage questions apply to the specific property. The Colorado Division of Insurance cautions that coverage summaries do not replace policy terms, conditions, special limits and exclusions. The documents and insurer's response are more useful than a universal assumption about condo coverage.
