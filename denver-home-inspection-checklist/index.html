Denver Home Inspection Checklist: What to Investigate

Use a focused inspection plan for a Denver-area purchase, track questions from the general inspection, and decide when specialist evaluation may help.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·9 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

A Denver home inspection checklist helps you turn a general inspection into a focused set of questions about the property’s condition and major components. The Colorado Division of Real Estate cautions that a home inspection is limited to general condition and major components; specialists may be appropriate for questions such as sewer or structural concerns. Use the inspection period and options stated in your signed contract, and treat findings as information to evaluate, not an automatic repair promise. Colorado Division of Real Estate home-buying guide.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • Prepare questions and access needs before the inspection, then record findings by location and level of concern.
  • Use a general inspection to understand broad condition, and consider a qualified specialist when a specific concern warrants deeper evaluation.
  • Check your signed contract for applicable inspection conditions, deadlines, and procedures instead of assuming a standard remedy.

Use the inspection to answer decisions, not collect labels

A useful inspection is not a hunt for a perfect home or a contest to produce the longest list. It is a structured opportunity to understand the property’s general condition, identify items that need more information, and decide which questions matter to your plans. Before the visit, write down what you need to learn and how you will use the information.

The Colorado Division of Real Estate explains that a home inspection is limited to general condition and major components, and that qualified specialists may be needed for sewer scopes or structural concerns. Read the Division’s home-buying guidance on inspection scope before treating a general report as a complete technical diagnosis. A report is a starting point for informed follow-up, not proof that every concealed or specialized issue has been evaluated.

Keep the transaction context in view. Inspection conditions, deadlines, and procedures depend on the signed contract. The Division notes that contracts may include inspection-related conditions and that the written language should specify deadlines and rights if a condition is not met or waived. Use the Division’s contract guidance to remind yourself to check the actual agreement, rather than relying on a generic checklist for legal timing.

Prepare a property-specific inspection plan

Before the inspection, gather what you already know from the listing, your visits, and available property records. Convert observations into neutral questions. Instead of deciding that something is defective based on appearance, note its location and ask the inspector to describe what is observable, what was not accessible, and whether additional evaluation may be warranted.

Exterior and siteWhat visible condition or drainage-related question should I understand?Record the location and whether an area could not be assessed
Roof and visible structureWhat can be observed, and is any specialist opinion suggested?Separate visible observations from conclusions not established by the visit
Interior spacesAre there observable conditions that deserve explanation or monitoring?Record the room, feature, and report reference
Plumbing and electrical featuresWhat was examined, and what is outside the inspection scope?List unanswered questions for the relevant specialist
Heating and other major componentsWhat general condition was observed and what information is missing?Note any recommendation for additional assessment
Basement or lower levelAre there concerns that merit a separate evaluation or test?Distinguish observation, measurement, and specialist evaluation

This is a planning structure, not an assertion that every property has each feature or that every feature can be fully evaluated during a general inspection. Adapt it to the home and your concerns. Keep a separate place for questions that need a specialist so they are not lost in a long report.

Ask what the general inspection does and does not cover

During the inspection, ask the inspector to explain the scope of the work, the areas that were accessible, and any limitations. Ask what an observation means in plain language and whether it is a finding, a recommendation for further evaluation, or an item that could not be assessed. This helps distinguish a concrete concern from a question that needs more evidence.

Do not treat every notation as an urgent defect, and do not dismiss an item because it appears in a standard report format. Ask how the inspector prioritized it and what information would help you understand it. If a report points to a specialized concern, determine whether a qualified professional can evaluate that particular issue and whether access or additional documentation is needed.

A general inspection does not replace specialist work for questions outside its scope. The Division specifically gives sewer scopes and structural concerns as examples where qualified specialists may be needed. The state’s buyer guide explains this distinction. Use the recommendation in the context of the finding and avoid treating an unperformed specialist evaluation as a confirmed result.

Choose specialist follow-up based on the question

Specialist evaluation is most useful when it answers a defined question. If the general inspection identifies a possible sewer concern, ask what a sewer scope would assess and what access or records are needed. If a structural issue is raised, ask what type of specialist is appropriate and what the inspection observation does and does not establish. The purpose is to investigate the specific concern, not to order every possible service without a reason.

Use a short decision record: concern, source, unanswered question, potential specialist, access needed, and the date by which you need information under your contract. The date should come from your signed agreement, not from a general article or a standard assumption. If access is uncertain, coordinate with the appropriate transaction contacts before scheduling a service.

Specialist reports may have different scopes from the general inspection. Read each report for what was examined, the limitations, and any next steps it recommends. Keep the report’s conclusions intact; do not turn a limited evaluation into a guarantee about unrelated parts of the home. If two reports appear inconsistent, ask the relevant professionals to clarify how their scopes differ.

Consider radon testing as a distinct question

Radon is a separate testing issue, not something to infer from a general inspection. Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment guidance encourages homebuyers to test during transaction inspections using a licensed radon measurement professional. For transactions, EPA recommends testing the lowest area that could become living space, such as an unfinished basement. Follow the professional’s or device’s instructions and arrange appropriate access and test conditions.

If a result is elevated, discuss mitigation with the seller and a qualified professional. The state guidance does not mean a seller automatically has to install a system or pay for mitigation. Review Colorado’s radon testing guidance for testing considerations. If a mitigation system is already present, testing still matters: CDPHE advises testing even when a system is installed and contacting a licensed mitigation professional for elevated readings and system evaluation. See the state’s radon FAQ.

Track the test’s location, conditions, instructions, and result as separate facts. Do not infer a property-specific result from the presence of equipment, the age of a system, or a prior conversation. If you need interpretation, use the licensed measurement or mitigation professional who can address the actual test and system.

Turn findings into a prioritized decision list

After the inspection, avoid treating every item as equally important. Sort the findings into categories that help you decide what to do next: needs clarification, possible specialist follow-up, maintenance or planning question, and no further action identified based on current information. These are organizational categories, not universal technical labels. A qualified inspector or specialist can explain the significance of a particular finding.

  1. Read the full report. Note the summary, detailed observations, limitations, and recommendations.
  2. Mark decision-relevant items. Identify findings that affect your comfort, plans, or need for more information.
  3. Write one question per item. Avoid vague requests such as “Is the house okay?”
  4. Decide whether more expertise is needed. Match a specialist to a specific concern, such as a sewer or structural question.
  5. Check the contract. Confirm applicable dates, notice procedures, and options in the signed agreement.
  6. Save the record. Keep the report and any follow-up evaluations with your transaction documents.

This helps you separate condition evaluation from negotiation. Colorado’s official forms include an Inspection Objection Notice and an Inspection Resolution, but their names alone do not establish substantive rights, deadlines, or an automatic outcome. Check the current official forms resource with your broker or attorney and use the version appropriate to the transaction.

Coordinate inspection questions with the contract

The inspection report is information; the contract defines the transaction’s agreed process. Review the inspection-related language and identify the dates and procedures that apply to you. Do not assume that a finding automatically requires a repair, gives you a particular remedy, or guarantees a deposit refund. The Division explains that earnest money amount and refund conditions depend on the signed sales contract, and that refund is not automatic or universal. Read the Division’s sales-contract overview and follow the actual written terms.

If you are considering an objection or resolution, focus first on the underlying facts: what the report says, what remains unknown, and what outcome you want to discuss. Keep communications clear and tied to the transaction documents. The precise rights and options depend on the agreement, so a checklist should not be mistaken for legal interpretation.

For broader purchase planning, the buying consultation page can help you prepare questions for your transaction team. If you are comparing the financial side of a purchase, see the guide to Denver buyer closing costs. Keep inspection decisions and financing estimates in separate records so an unresolved property question does not get confused with a cash-to-close estimate.

Questions to ask before deciding what comes next

What did the inspector actually observe? What areas or components were not accessible or outside the inspection scope? Which findings need a specialist to answer a specific question? Is radon testing being arranged with a licensed professional and appropriate conditions? What do the signed contract terms say about the relevant dates and procedures? Which documents or explanations are still missing?

  • Use neutral questions that invite clarification rather than assume a diagnosis.
  • Match specialist evaluations to specific concerns.
  • Keep testing results and equipment information distinct.
  • Use the signed contract, not a generic checklist, for transaction procedures.

A focused inspection process gives you a clearer basis for deciding whether you need more information and how the property fits your plans. Record what is known, preserve the limits of each report, and resolve contract questions through the appropriate transaction professionals before relying on an assumed outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Does a home inspection cover every problem with a property?+

No. The Colorado Division of Real Estate describes a home inspection as limited to general condition and major components. It notes that qualified specialists may be needed for questions such as sewer scopes or structural concerns. Ask what was accessible, what was outside the inspection scope, and whether a particular finding needs further evaluation. Treat the general report as useful information, not as a guarantee that every issue has been found.

Should I arrange radon testing during a home purchase?+

Colorado’s Department of Public Health and Environment encourages homebuyers to test during transaction inspections using a licensed radon measurement professional. Follow the professional’s or device’s instructions and arrange appropriate access and test conditions. CDPHE also advises testing even if a mitigation system is already installed. If a reading is elevated, discuss it with a qualified professional and seller; the guidance does not mean the seller automatically must install or pay for mitigation.

Does an inspection finding mean the seller must make a repair?+

An inspection finding does not by itself establish a repair obligation or a particular transaction outcome. The applicable process and options depend on the signed contract. Review the inspection-related terms, dates, and procedures in that agreement, and discuss the actual report and any follow-up evaluation with your transaction team. Colorado’s official forms include inspection-related forms, but their names alone do not establish rights, deadlines, or automatic remedies.
