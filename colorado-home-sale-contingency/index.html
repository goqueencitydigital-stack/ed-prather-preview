Colorado Home Sale Contingencies: Coordinate Your Next Move

Plan a Colorado home sale contingency with a clear timeline, financing questions and decision points for coordinating your current sale with a next purchase.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·11 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

A Colorado home sale contingency connects a purchase to the sale of an existing home, but the contract must specify what needs to happen, by when and what choices follow if it does not. Sellers considering a contingent offer should evaluate the buyer’s sale progress, financing, deadlines and backup plan, rather than treating the contingency label as a complete explanation. Buyers coordinating both moves should model the timing and cash consequences before committing. Colorado Division of Real Estate sales-contract guidance.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • A home sale contingency’s effect comes from the signed contract terms, including conditions, deadlines, notices and consequences if unmet.
  • Assess the buyer’s current sale using verifiable milestones and a written timeline rather than assumptions about a likely closing.
  • Plan how you would proceed if the sale is delayed, the purchase timing changes or your next home is no longer available.

A home sale contingency can connect two major transactions: a buyer’s purchase of a next home and the sale of the home that may fund or enable it. For a Colorado seller considering a contingent offer, the key question is not merely whether a buyer has a home to sell. It is whether the offer’s written terms, evidence of sale progress and timing fit your own plans.

Colorado contracts may contain conditions tied to an existing-home sale, financing, appraisal, inspection and other matters. The Division of Real Estate explains that contract language should specify what must happen, deadlines, rights if a condition is unmet or waived, and possible default remedies. Review the state’s contract guidance and evaluate the actual agreement rather than assuming a contingency has a standard meaning.

This guide is designed for sellers deciding how to assess and coordinate an offer, while also helping buyers prepare a credible plan for selling and moving. It offers a process for questions, comparison and scheduling, not a guaranteed outcome.

Map the two transactions before judging the offer

Start with a visual sequence of events: the buyer’s existing-home sale, the purchase of your home, your own move or replacement-home plan, and the closing and possession dates that depend on them. A home sale contingency can create a chain of dependencies. If one date moves, the next step may need to move too. Writing the chain down exposes conflicts that are easy to miss when considering only the offer price.

Ask the buyer for a clear description of the current home’s sale status. Useful milestones include whether it is being prepared for market, actively offered, under contract or approaching closing. Request dates and supporting details the buyer is comfortable sharing, such as a relevant contract milestone or updated expected closing date. Do not treat any status as a guarantee that a sale will complete.

As the seller, compare that information with your own constraints. Do you need predictable proceeds for another purchase? Have you made a commitment to move? Is possession timing more important than a particular closing date? Which expenses or arrangements depend on the sale being completed by a certain point? A sell-and-buy planning resource can help organize linked tasks, but the signed purchase agreement controls the transaction.

Read the contingency as a set of questions

A contingency is not one universal package. Review the specific language for what event must occur, what evidence or notice is needed, when a decision is due and what the parties may do if the event does not occur. Look for language addressing the buyer’s existing home, the purchase, deadlines, notice, amendments, termination and any default provisions. Check the complete agreement, not just the paragraph or form title.

Colorado’s Division of Real Estate describes existing-home-sale conditions as one kind of contract condition and says terms should define what must happen and the rights and consequences if the condition is not met or waived. Use its overview as a framework for contract review, then have the actual wording explained in context.

Questions to resolve include:

  • What exact event satisfies the buyer’s home sale condition?
  • Is the condition tied to a sale contract, closing, receipt of funds or another defined event?
  • What evidence or written notice must be provided, and by whom?
  • What are the decision dates, and how are any extensions documented?
  • What choices does the agreement provide if the buyer’s sale is delayed or does not occur?
  • How do financing, appraisal and other conditions interact with the home sale provision?

Do not assume that a general phrase such as sale of buyer’s home answers these questions. The state notes that a sales contract is legally binding and includes deadlines; ask for clarification before accepting language you do not understand. If a proposed change is made, preserve the signed written amendment and update your calendar.

Evaluate progress with a neutral evidence checklist

To compare contingent and non-contingent offers, use consistent criteria rather than a hunch about how quickly a buyer can sell. A simple checklist can help distinguish evidence from optimism. Ask for only the information needed to evaluate timing and performance, and handle personal financial or transaction details appropriately.

Ask the buyer or their representative to distinguish confirmed facts from estimates. An expected sale date is a planning input, not certainty. Revisit the checklist when there is a meaningful change, and keep a written record of what has been updated. This makes a later decision easier to explain and reduces reliance on assumptions remembered from a conversation.

Compare the contingent offer against alternatives on more than price. Consider financing terms, closing timing, possession requests, other conditions, the buyer’s demonstrated progress and your tolerance for uncertainty. There is no one factor that establishes which offer is best for every seller.

Build a timeline with decision gates

Use decision gates to set moments when you will check whether the plan still works. A gate is a scheduled review, not an invented contract deadline. Put the contractual dates first, then add practical check-ins before them so you have time to request information and discuss options. Ask your broker to identify which dates are binding and which are simply planning reminders.

For example, a timeline worksheet might have columns for milestone, contract date, expected evidence, responsible person and next decision. Include the buyer’s home-sale progress, the lender’s financing updates, your own moving arrangements and any possession agreement being considered. If the buyer’s sale moves, update the dependent dates rather than letting the original plan remain in circulation.

Where possible, avoid making an irreversible commitment based only on a projected closing. If you are arranging a move, ask vendors about flexible scheduling and confirm cancellation or change terms directly. If you need sale proceeds for another purchase, coordinate the lender’s cash-to-close estimate and expected transaction timing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that estimated cash to close accounts for the down payment, closing costs, deposits, credits and adjustments. Its Loan Estimate guide can help a buyer organize those figures, but it does not establish whether a specific sale will close on time.

Compare the contingency with the seller’s alternatives

Rather than asking whether to accept or reject a contingency in the abstract, compare practical choices. You might accept the offer as written, negotiate different dates or terms, request more information, or choose another offer if one is available and better fits your priorities. The available options depend on the offer, market circumstances known to you and applicable contract documents; do not assume a particular right from the contingency label.

Use a side-by-side comparison that includes your preferred possession date, net-proceeds planning, certainty of timing and flexibility. Avoid assigning a value to a buyer’s home sale based on an unsupported estimate. If you need to compare likely sale proceeds, use a transparent valuation process and clearly label it as an estimate rather than a guaranteed amount. Your home valuation resource can support a discussion about your own property’s estimated value, not promise a buyer’s sale price.

Coordinate financing, proceeds and earnest money questions

A buyer may depend on proceeds from an existing home, yet the seller should not infer from that fact alone that the buyer lacks financing or that a particular deposit is at risk. Ask the buyer’s representative to explain the relevant financing and sale dependencies within appropriate boundaries. The buyer should get current lender figures and understand what happens if expected proceeds differ from the plan.

Earnest money terms are governed by the signed contract. Colorado’s Division of Real Estate says earnest money represents buyer good faith, is generally held by a title company, and has refund conditions that depend on the agreement. See the state’s buyer guidance. As a seller, do not assume the deposit is automatically forfeited if the buyer’s home does not sell, or automatically returned if a transaction ends. Review the relevant contract conditions and release process.

Buyers who plan to use sale proceeds should ask their lender how the timing and amount of those proceeds affect the purchase funds needed. Closing-cost estimates may change as transaction details change. The CFPB advises comparing Loan Estimates of the same type and checking costs, credits and cash-to-close estimates. Review the CFPB’s Loan Estimate explainer for a framework to discuss with the lender.

For sellers, proceeds planning is also about timing: when funds are expected, what costs must be paid and whether another transaction depends on them. Make a conservative schedule and identify what you would do if the anticipated date shifts.

Plan for delay, changed plans and possession

Before accepting an offer, discuss a delay plan. If the buyer’s sale takes longer than expected, would you consider an extension? Would you continue marketing or consider another arrangement? The answer should be based on the contract, the parties’ agreement and your own commitments. Avoid assuming that a seller has a particular right to continue marketing or that a buyer has a particular right to extend; confirm the actual terms.

Possession may not occur at the same time as closing if the parties negotiate a different arrangement. Colorado’s Division of Real Estate lists a Post-Closing Occupancy Agreement among its official forms, but the form’s existence does not establish automatic permission or a standard set of terms. Check the current state form resources and have any proposed occupancy arrangement reviewed in context.

If you expect to remain after closing or need to coordinate with a buyer’s move, clarify the dates, responsibilities, insurance questions, property condition and payment terms in the actual agreement. Do not rely on a casual understanding about keys, utilities or access. If your next purchase depends on a specific date, build in a backup plan for temporary storage or alternate arrangements where feasible, and confirm all costs directly rather than relying on assumptions.

The aim is to make flexibility explicit. A written plan can protect the relationship between the parties by making expectations visible before a problem arises.

Use a seller’s decision sequence

When a contingent offer arrives, work through the same sequence each time. This helps you balance the buyer’s situation with your own needs, while reserving legal interpretation for the actual contract and qualified guidance.

  1. List your non-negotiable timing needs, proceeds dependencies and possession preferences.
  2. Ask for the buyer’s current home-sale status, next milestone, expected dates and financing dependencies.
  3. Read the contingency and related conditions for events, deadlines, notices and outcomes.
  4. Compare the offer with alternatives using the same criteria, including price, timing, conditions and flexibility.
  5. Identify a delay plan and determine whether any proposed changes need to be written into the agreement.
  6. Put contractual dates and practical check-ins on a shared calendar, naming a person responsible for each follow-up.
  7. Reassess when a milestone changes, using updated information rather than the initial forecast.

A home sale contingency does not have to be treated as either an automatic rejection or a harmless detail. It is a coordination problem that can be evaluated through clear evidence, contract terms and your own priorities. When the timeline is explicit and each party understands what happens next, you can make a more informed decision about whether the proposed sequence fits your move.

Frequently asked questions

Does a Colorado home sale contingency have a standard meaning?+

Do not assume it does. Colorado contracts may contain conditions tied to an existing-home sale, but the signed contract should specify what must happen, deadlines, rights if a condition is unmet or waived, and possible consequences. Review the exact language and how it interacts with financing, appraisal and other conditions. A familiar label cannot substitute for checking the full agreement, required notices and dates that apply to the specific offer.

What should a seller ask a buyer who needs to sell a home first?+

Ask for the current sale stage, the next expected milestone, the timing the buyer is relying on, and which parts of the purchase depend on that sale. Clarify what evidence can be shared and what the buyer’s plan is if the sale is delayed. Then compare those details with your own proceeds, move and possession needs. Treat projected dates as planning estimates, not guarantees, and confirm all relevant duties and choices in the contract.

Can a seller accept another offer while a home sale contingency is in place?+

The answer depends on the actual signed agreement and applicable circumstances; do not infer a right from the fact that a buyer has a home sale contingency. Review the contract for any provisions addressing other offers, notices, deadlines and the parties’ choices. Ask your broker or attorney to explain the specific wording before acting. Any change or agreement should be properly documented, and the timing should be coordinated with the parties involved.
