Explore condominium homes.

Start with the details that matter to your ownership experience, then compare real properties with your agent.

Explore Colorado · A practical guide

A condominium purchase involves more than evaluating the individual unit. Review the association’s records, financial plans, insurance information, and maintenance practices, then confirm how parking, storage, and shared facilities work in practice. Use these steps to organize questions for the seller, association, inspector, and other relevant professionals.

01

Start With the Association Records

A condominium’s shared responsibilities make association records an important part of buyer due diligence. Once you are under contract as a buyer, the Colorado residential purchase contract entitles you to the association documents listed in section 7. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Ask your broker how to obtain the complete package, then set aside time to read it rather than relying only on a summary.

Begin with the declaration, bylaws, rules, current budget, financial statements, meeting minutes, insurance information, and management details if available. Use a simple tracker: document received, date, questions, and who will follow up. Flag conflicting information, missing attachments, unclear responsibility for repairs, or rules that affect how you expect to use the unit. Ask for clarification before your review period ends, as applicable to your transaction.

02

Read the Budget and Ask About Reserves

Look beyond the monthly assessment when reviewing the association’s finances. The Colorado Division of Real Estate recommends reviewing both governing and financial records to understand an HOA’s operations and financial condition. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Compare the current budget with recent financial statements and ask what the reserve balance is intended to cover, how the board estimates future work, and whether planned projects are reflected in the records.

Prepare specific questions rather than trying to infer a building’s condition from one number. Ask when the reserve study or other long-term planning document was last updated, what major work is anticipated, and whether estimates or bids are available. If a document is absent, ask whether it exists and how the board plans for the expense. A qualified accountant or reserve-study professional can help you understand records, without replacing your own review.

03

Understand Insurance and Repair Responsibilities

Ask for the association’s current insurance declarations and a plain-language explanation of what the policy covers, what it excludes, and which deductibles may apply. Colorado law requires associations to maintain specified property insurance on common elements and commercial general liability coverage related to common elements. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Confirm details with the association and an insurance professional rather than assuming the association policy covers everything inside your unit.

Map out who handles repairs for the unit, building systems, and shared spaces. Ask the manager or board for the governing documents that describe maintenance boundaries, plus any recent insurance notices or claims information they can provide. For attached units, the state advises buyers to pay particular attention to whether appropriate association coverage is maintained. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Discuss personal coverage needs with an insurance professional.

04

Check Assessments and Meeting Minutes

Regular dues may not be the only association expense to consider. The Colorado Division of Real Estate notes that special assessments, insurance-related costs, and other unexpected expenses may arise, and recommends reviewing the prior year’s meeting minutes for special assessments under consideration. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Read minutes alongside the budget and financial statements so proposed projects and recurring costs have context.

Create a cost-and-question worksheet for the seller or association: current regular assessment, what it funds, any announced assessment, payment schedule, and projects discussed but not yet approved. Ask whether the amount has changed recently and what services or utilities are included. Do not treat an informal verbal estimate as a substitute for written association records. If the documents leave a material question unresolved, request a written explanation and review it with your transaction professionals.

05

Confirm Parking and Storage Details

Do not assume a parking space or storage area is included just because it is shown during a tour or mentioned casually. Ask the seller to identify each space or locker and request the association records that describe its status and permitted use. Compare the records with the unit’s sale materials, and ask the association to explain any unclear or inconsistent descriptions.

Test the practical details that matter to your routine: exact location, access method, height or size limits, guest arrangements, charging or utility access, and rules for changing how the space is used. Ask who handles maintenance and whom to contact if access fails. If a feature is essential to your purchase decision, get its description and any relevant restrictions in writing before relying on it.

06

Evaluate Day-to-Day Building Operations

Observe shared areas during your visits and note the condition of entrances, hallways, elevators, waste areas, exterior grounds, and any amenities you expect to use. Colorado’s guidance explains that the executive board determines how and when association maintenance and landscaping are performed. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Compare what you see with the maintenance responsibilities and plans described in the association documents.

Ask whether the association uses a community association manager and what work the manager performs. The state describes community association managers as agents for the executive board who generally assist with day-to-day operations, with duties varying by community and management agreement. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Request the manager’s contact information, ask how residents report issues, and note whether replies to general questions are clear and timely.

07

Separate Unit Inspection From Document Review

Association records and a unit inspection answer different questions. Treat the document review as a way to understand shared finances, rules, insurance, and maintenance responsibilities; arrange an appropriate inspection to assess the unit’s visible condition and systems. Make a separate list of concerns from the tour, such as signs of moisture, unusual odors, noisy equipment, or fixtures that do not operate as expected.

Ask your inspector which observations warrant follow-up by a specialist, and coordinate access for any additional evaluation you choose to arrange. If radon is relevant to your transaction, Colorado’s health department lists a radon brochure for real estate transactions and resources on measurement and mitigation. Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment: Radon and Real Estate Keep inspection findings, association records, and seller responses organized as separate records.

Questions & answers.

Which condominium association documents should I request?+

Start with the governing and financial documents available under section 7 of the Colorado residential purchase contract, then ask for the current budget, financial statements, meeting minutes, insurance information, and management contact details. The state advises buyers to review association documents to understand operations and financial health. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Track what you receive and follow up on gaps.

How can I find out whether a special assessment is being discussed?+

Read the association’s meeting minutes, especially those from the past year, alongside its budget and financial records. Colorado’s real estate division specifically recommends checking minutes for special assessments that may be under consideration. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Ask the association to clarify whether a discussed expense is proposed, approved, or already due.

Does the association’s insurance cover everything in my unit?+

Do not assume that it does. Colorado requires specified association coverage for common elements, but your unit’s coverage and responsibilities depend on the applicable documents and policy details. Colorado Division of Real Estate: Buying a Home in a HOA Request the policy declarations and ask the association to explain boundaries and deductibles. Discuss your own coverage with an insurance professional.

How do I verify that parking or storage comes with the unit?+

Ask the seller to identify the specific space or locker, then compare that description with the association’s governing records and sale documents. Confirm access, permitted use, restrictions, and any maintenance responsibilities in writing. If the records are unclear or inconsistent, ask the association or its manager to explain the discrepancy before treating the space as an included or essential feature.
