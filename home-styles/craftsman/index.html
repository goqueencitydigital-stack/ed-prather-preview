Explore Craftsman homes.

Start with the details that matter to your ownership experience, then compare real properties with your agent.

Explore Colorado · A practical guide

A thoughtful Craftsman search starts with more than a style label. Compare the home’s overall form with its details, inspect the porch and surviving woodwork, look closely at alterations and moisture clues, and request records that help you plan an informed next step.

Updated November 6, 2026

01

Separate bungalow form from Craftsman details

Start by distinguishing a home’s overall form from its stylistic details. History Colorado describes the bungalow as the most common expression of Craftsman architecture, while noting that bungalow examples also appear in Mission, Pueblo Revival, and Mediterranean styles. The terms can describe different aspects of a home, so avoid treating them as interchangeable. History Colorado’s bungalow overview

At a showing, note the house’s shape and scale separately from its finish details. Ask yourself whether the floor plan and exterior form suit your needs, then consider whether the visible features create the character you want. This two-part assessment helps you compare homes consistently, even when listing descriptions use “bungalow” and “Craftsman” loosely.

02

Look closely at the porch and approach

A front porch is a useful place to begin your visual inspection. History Colorado describes the typical bungalow as a one- or one-and-one-half-story home, often with a broad porch; it notes that almost all bungalows have a front porch. Check the porch’s condition, how it meets the house, and whether its scale and layout work for your daily use. History Colorado’s bungalow overview

Look beyond first impressions: note loose or damaged materials, areas that appear recently patched, and whether the porch structure seems to have been changed. These are observations, not a substitute for an inspection. Photograph the details and ask the seller what work was done, when it occurred, and whether invoices, permits, or contractor records are available.

03

Compare woodwork, lines, and scale

When considering Craftsman character, look for a coordinated composition rather than relying on one decorative feature. History Colorado’s bungalow description identifies simple horizontal lines, overhanging eaves, and, in some examples, exposed rafter ends. It also describes porch supports that may be battered piers or thick columns. These details can help you compare what is present with what you value. History Colorado’s bungalow overview

Make a short preference list before touring: which visible details matter most, which are optional, and which changes would affect your decision? Check whether interior woodwork, trim, or other features you appreciate appear consistent from room to room. If a feature is partly concealed or looks newly installed, ask for its history rather than assuming it is original.

04

Understand alterations and surviving material

A home’s present appearance may reflect several rounds of work. During a tour, separate what you can observe from what you can verify: record changes to openings, porch areas, trim, or room layouts, then ask which changes were made by the current owner and which predate their ownership. Request photographs, plans, permits, or contractor paperwork when available.

Decide what “original” means for your own priorities. You may care most about a particular feature, a coherent appearance, or the freedom to update the home; those are different preferences. Avoid treating an old-looking finish as proof of age. If a feature strongly affects your decision, ask an appropriate building or preservation professional how it can be assessed.

05

Investigate moisture clues before deciding

Give visible moisture clues a deliberate second look, especially around the porch and exterior areas where materials meet. Note staining, peeling finishes, soft or damaged surfaces, musty odors, or recent patching, but do not try to diagnose a cause from appearance alone. Photograph concerns and ask the seller whether there have been leaks, repairs, or recurring problems.

If you see signs that concern you, make a qualified inspection part of your due diligence and ask the inspector to explain the observed condition, likely next steps, and any limits on what could be examined. Where appropriate, consult a moisture or building-envelope specialist. Keep the written findings and compare them with repair records before deciding how to proceed.

06

Request records and distinguish them from inspections

Ask for available records tied to the property’s maintenance and changes: permits, plans, invoices, warranties, repair history, and reports from prior inspections. Use them to form questions, not as a replacement for your own evaluation. A record may document a past project without establishing the current condition of the work or materials.

Radon is a separate due-diligence item from a style preference. The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment lists a radon brochure for Colorado real estate transactions and rental agreements, along with measuring and mitigation guidance. Review the current state resources, ask what radon information is available for the property, and consider whether testing is appropriate. Colorado’s radon and real estate resources

07

Check local requirements and plan your next steps

If you are considering exterior changes, additions, or other work, check requirements for the property’s actual location before making plans. Denver describes zoning as regulating property use and development, including the size and location of structures and allowed activities. That page is Denver-specific, not a statewide guide; visitors considering homes elsewhere should verify rules with their own local jurisdiction. Denver zoning permits and information

For each home you compare, keep a simple file with your feature preferences, photos, seller answers, available records, and questions for inspectors. Separate items you like from issues that need professional review. If you use an external home search, its results depend on the tool and the criteria available; confirm property details and current status through the listing source and the people handling the transaction.

Questions & answers.

Is every bungalow a Craftsman home?+

No. History Colorado describes the bungalow as the most common expression of Craftsman architecture, but also notes bungalow examples in Mission, Pueblo Revival, and Mediterranean styles. Treat the house’s overall form and its stylistic details as separate things to evaluate, rather than relying on a single label. History Colorado’s bungalow overview

What should I look at first during a Craftsman home tour?+

Start with the overall form, then look at the porch, visible woodwork, roof edges, and any changes that stand out. Photograph features you want to ask about and note signs of patching or damage. Your observations help guide questions, but they do not replace a qualified inspection or establish a feature’s age.

Does a bungalow’s style mean it has original features?+

No. A style label alone does not verify when a feature was installed or whether it has been altered. Ask the seller for photographs, plans, permits, invoices, or other available records. If a particular detail matters to your decision, ask an appropriate building or preservation professional how it could be assessed.

Where can I find Colorado radon information for a real estate transaction?+

The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment provides radon resources for real estate, including a brochure for transactions and rental agreements and guidance on measuring and mitigation. Review the current state materials, ask what information is available for the specific property, and consider whether testing is appropriate. Colorado’s radon and real estate resources

Do Denver zoning requirements apply to every Colorado home?+

No. The cited zoning page describes Denver’s rules and services, not statewide requirements. Denver zoning permits and information If you are considering work on a property outside Denver, verify applicable zoning and permit requirements with the jurisdiction where that property is located. Check before planning exterior changes, additions, or other development.
