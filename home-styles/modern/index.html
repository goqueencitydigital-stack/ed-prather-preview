Explore modern homes.

Start with the details that matter to your ownership experience, then compare real properties with your agent.

Explore Colorado · A practical guide

A modern label can describe a look, but it does not tell you how a home was built or maintained. Compare how glazing, daylight, shade, privacy, roof details and indoor-outdoor layouts work for your needs, then check records and arrange appropriate inspections before drawing conclusions about the property.

Updated November 6, 2026

01

Start with the house, not the label

Treat “modern” as a starting point for describing a listing, not proof of its age, construction method, upkeep or present condition. First write down what you want the design to do: bring light into particular rooms, connect living spaces to a patio, or offer privacy from neighboring windows. Then compare the actual rooms, openings and sightlines rather than relying on a style tag.

If a home is described with a more specific architectural term, ask what visible features support that description and whether its history is documented. Colorado’s selected post-World War II residential architecture guide discusses Usonian design as one postwar style and identifies features including integrated windows, horizontal lines and inside-outside walls. That is a specific example, not a definition or guarantee for every home marketed as modern. Colorado postwar residential architecture guide

02

Read the glazing and daylight

Visit at a time when you can assess the rooms you care about, and note which windows bring in useful light and which create glare or make screens hard to see. Compare the same spaces at different times if possible. Look at the view from seated and standing positions, and consider whether furniture placement would block a window or interrupt the way you use the room.

Ask what shading is already provided and how you would manage sunlight through the day. Check whether window coverings, exterior shade or landscaping are present, included, and in working order; confirm details rather than assuming they remain with the home. If the listing does not explain glazing or window work, request available product, installation and repair records, and make a list of questions for an inspector.

03

Test privacy and indoor-outdoor flow

Walk the route between the main living area and any deck, patio or yard. Check the door’s operation, the transition underfoot, and whether the layout suits how you expect to move between spaces. Stand inside and outside to assess views into the home, exposure to nearby activity and whether the outdoor area feels usable for your routines.

For large windows or open layouts, test privacy in daylight and after dark, when interior lighting can change what is visible from outside. Ask which window coverings or privacy features are included and whether they fit the openings. Consider where you would place seating, screens or other furnishings, and whether those choices would reduce daylight or obstruct the indoor-outdoor route.

04

Look closely at roofs and edges

A roofline may be part of the design you like, but appearance alone cannot answer questions about materials, installation or maintenance. Ask what the roof covering is, when it was installed or repaired, and whether the seller has invoices, permits, warranties or inspection records. During a visit, note visible concerns and ask a qualified inspector to assess areas that cannot be checked safely from the ground.

Ask how roof edges, window openings, doors and the transitions to exterior spaces have been maintained. Request records of repairs and any recurring water entry, and compare the seller’s answers with available documents and inspection findings. If a detail is hidden or inaccessible, record it as unresolved rather than assuming it is sound because the finish looks clean or the listing uses a design-forward description.

05

Verify construction and radon information

Ask for records that help explain how the home was built and altered: permits where applicable, renovation descriptions, product information, warranties and maintenance history. If a seller or listing mentions a radon system, request its documentation and ask what testing or service records are available. Colorado provides a required radon brochure for real-estate transactions and rental agreements, along with guidance on measuring and mitigating radon. Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment: Radon and real estate

For new construction, ask whether radon-resistant features were installed and what documentation describes the system; do not infer their presence from a home’s appearance. Colorado’s health department lists resources on radon control techniques in new homes and technical guidance on measuring and mitigating radon. Discuss testing and any results with an appropriately qualified professional, and keep the question about radon separate from your preferences for glazing, layout or finishes. Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment: Radon and real estate

06

Compare listings and check local requirements

Use a search, including an external COHouseHunters search if helpful, to find candidates rather than treating results as confirmation of current availability or complete property information. Listing labels and photographs cannot establish construction details or condition. Open each listing, check the details provided, and ask for missing records; compare homes using the same notes on daylight, shade, privacy, roof documentation and indoor-outdoor use.

If a renovation or addition matters to your plans, verify applicable requirements for the property’s actual location before relying on assumptions. Denver’s zoning information says zoning regulates matters such as structure size and location and directs users to look up a property’s zone district. That information is Denver-specific, not a statewide rule; visitors considering homes elsewhere should check with the relevant local jurisdiction. City and County of Denver: Zoning Permits

Before your next move

Questions & answers.

Does a modern listing label mean the home is in good condition?+

No. A style or marketing label describes presentation, not verified construction quality or maintenance. Ask for available repair and renovation records, note unanswered questions, and arrange appropriate inspections. Evaluate the home’s current condition from evidence rather than assuming the label tells you how well the property has been maintained.

What should I check about large windows?+

Consider daylight, glare, views, privacy and how you would shade each room at different times. Ask what window coverings or other shade features are included, whether they work, and whether records exist for window installation or repairs. An inspection can help address condition questions that a viewing cannot resolve.

Should I ask about radon in a modern home?+

Yes. Ask whether testing has been done and request any available results or system records. Colorado provides a radon brochure for real-estate transactions and rental agreements, as well as resources about testing, mitigation and radon-resistant construction in new homes. Consult the state guidance and a qualified professional about testing questions. Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment: Radon and real estate

Can I use Denver zoning information for a home elsewhere in Colorado?+

No. Denver’s zoning page explains Denver’s own zoning information and property lookup process. For a property in another city or county, check the jurisdiction that governs that address. If a planned change matters to your decision, verify the applicable local requirements with the relevant authority before assuming it is allowed. City and County of Denver: Zoning Permits
