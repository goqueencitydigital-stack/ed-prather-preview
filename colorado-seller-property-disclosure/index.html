Colorado Seller Property Disclosures: A Preparation Guide

Prepare your Colorado seller property disclosure with a records checklist, careful wording, current forms and questions for your real estate transaction team.

By Ed Prather Real Estate·November 4, 2026·10 min read

The essentials

The short answer.

Preparing a Colorado seller property disclosure is best approached as a careful information-gathering task. The Colorado Division of Real Estate publishes a Seller’s Property Disclosure form, but the form’s existence alone does not establish a particular disclosure duty or answer what applies to an individual sale. Organize records, review the current applicable form with your transaction team, and distinguish personal knowledge from guesses. For most pre-1978 housing, federal lead-disclosure rules apply, subject to exemptions and specific requirements. Colorado Division of Real Estate contracts and forms.

Keep these priorities in mind

  • Gather records and make a clear inventory before completing the applicable disclosure form.
  • Separate what you personally know, what a record says, and what remains uncertain; do not guess or overstate certainty.
  • Treat federal lead-based paint disclosure as a distinct rule for most housing built before 1978, with exemptions and specific requirements.

Treat preparation as an information project

A property disclosure form is not a substitute for a thoughtful review of what you know about the home. Preparing well means collecting relevant records, organizing your recollections, identifying uncertainty, and checking that your answers are consistent with the information you have. It does not mean trying to diagnose every component or turning an assumption into a fact.

The Colorado Division of Real Estate lists a Seller’s Property Disclosure (Residential) among its official forms. That listing establishes that an official form exists; by itself, it does not establish a specific disclosure mandate, exemption, or answer for every seller or property. Check the Division’s current contract and forms resource with your broker or attorney to identify the applicable version and how it fits your transaction.

Keep three categories visible as you prepare: direct personal knowledge, information found in a document or report, and items you cannot confirm. This discipline makes the process more accurate and helps you avoid answering beyond what you know. If a question is unclear, record it for follow-up rather than filling the gap with a guess.

Gather records before answering questions

Start with a broad inventory of information already in your possession. The goal is not to claim that every record exists or that every seller must produce a particular document. It is to locate useful material you already have and make it available for review where relevant. Create a folder with clear file names and dates, and note whether a document covers the whole property or only a particular project, location, or period.

Information to look forPreparation questionHow to label it
Prior inspection or specialist reportsWhat area or component did the report address?Record the date, author, and scope without expanding its conclusions
Repair or service recordsWhat work is described, and is there supporting paperwork?Separate documented work from recollection
Product or system documentsDo you have manuals, warranties, or service information?Identify the item and any limits shown in the document
Permits or project recordsWhat records are actually available to you?Do not infer approval or completeness from a partial file
Insurance or association materialsDoes the document relate to the property or a shared area?Keep the document’s scope and source visible

These are organizational prompts, not a claim that each category is legally required or relevant in every sale. If you do not have a record, do not create a substitute explanation that implies you do. A simple index can say “not located” or “not known to seller” and preserve the distinction between missing paperwork and a confirmed condition.

Separate memory, documents, and uncertainty

Before writing an answer, ask where the information comes from. Is it something you personally observed? Does a dated report say it? Are you relying on a prior owner’s statement, a repair invoice, or a casual conversation? Each source carries different context, so preserve that context rather than collapsing it into a confident conclusion.

Use careful notes while reviewing the form. For each topic, write down what you know, the source, and any limit on that knowledge. For example, a report may cover one area and one date, while a repair record may document a specific service without establishing the condition of the entire system. Your preparation should not broaden either document beyond its stated scope.

If the form uses a term you do not understand, flag it and obtain an explanation before selecting an answer. The Division of Real Estate says brokers disclose adverse material facts actually known and should advise expert input beyond their expertise. The Division’s home-buying guide describes the broker’s role and the limits of general inspection information; use it as context, not as a complete statement of seller disclosure law.

Use the correct form and version thoughtfully

Official forms can change, and the Division’s forms page lists versions with different effective dates, including versions for transactions on or after January 1, 2026, as well as educational versions for 2027. Do not assume that a copy downloaded earlier is the one for your transaction. Ask the transaction team to identify the correct current form and explain how it should be completed in your circumstances. Use the official forms page to check the available versions.

Read the form in full before entering responses. Notice whether a question asks about your knowledge, a specific location, a past event, or a current condition. A response should answer the actual question as presented, not a broader or narrower version you have imagined. If you need additional space, ask how the transaction team wants supporting information attached or referenced rather than changing the form informally.

Keep a copy of the completed form and the records you used to prepare it. If you later remember a relevant fact or find a document that changes your understanding, do not quietly alter a saved copy without noting the change. Bring the new information to the appropriate professional and ask how it should be handled in the transaction.

Keep federal lead disclosure separate

Federal lead-based paint disclosure is a distinct topic from a general property disclosure form. The EPA says the federal rule applies to most housing built before 1978, with exemptions. Before buyers sign, the required pamphlet, known lead information, available records or reports, and a warning or disclosure statement must be provided. Buyers have an inspection opportunity subject to the rule and written arrangements; do not rely on an invented or generalized deadline.

The disclosure process is not a lead test and does not guarantee that a property is free of lead. If the home’s age or transaction circumstances raise questions, check current EPA guidance for applicable exceptions and timing. Read the EPA’s real estate lead-disclosure guidance and coordinate the applicable documents and process with your transaction team.

For this topic, keep the factual record precise: what is known, what records are available, and what the applicable process calls for. Do not turn “no report located” into “tested and clear,” and do not infer a property result from the age of a home. A disclosure statement and a test answer different questions.

Prepare a consistent, reviewable file

Consistency is easier when you compare the form with your own records before sharing it. Look for a repair invoice that appears to conflict with an answer, an old report that has been overlooked, or a document whose scope has been misunderstood. A discrepancy does not automatically resolve itself; it is a prompt to clarify what the record actually says and how it relates to your knowledge.

  1. Collect what you have. Save relevant reports, correspondence, receipts, product information, and other property records in one organized folder.
  2. Record the source. Note whether each statement comes from personal observation, a document, or another person’s account.
  3. Mark uncertainty. List questions you cannot answer confidently instead of guessing.
  4. Check the form version. Confirm that the transaction team has identified the applicable official form.
  5. Review for consistency. Compare responses with records and flag conflicts or incomplete context.
  6. Keep a final copy. Save the completed form and the supporting information used to prepare it.

This process is useful whether you are completing the form for the first time or checking information before it is delivered. If you are preparing to sell, the selling consultation page can help you organize transaction questions. For planning the financial side separately, consider the guide to Colorado seller closing costs.

Handle questions without making unsupported claims

When a buyer or representative asks about a condition, answer within the scope of what you know and the documents available. If you need to check a record, say that you are verifying it rather than improvising an answer. If a technical question requires interpretation, involve a qualified specialist whose work addresses that question. The purpose is to clarify information, not to guarantee a future condition.

A report or repair record should be described accurately. Avoid rewriting a specialist’s conclusion in stronger language, presenting a limited inspection as a whole-house certification, or describing a past repair as proof that no concern remains. If the document is ambiguous, preserve the original and ask the appropriate professional to explain its scope.

Keep a record of significant follow-up questions and the documents or explanations provided. If the answer changes because you found new information, communicate that promptly through the transaction process. The Division of Real Estate identifies an Inspection Objection Notice and Inspection Resolution among its official forms, but form names alone do not establish a right, deadline, or outcome. Confirm the applicable forms and terms with the transaction professionals rather than inferring their effect from their titles.

A practical final review before delivery

Before you submit or confirm a disclosure package, ask yourself: Did I use the form version identified for this transaction? Have I answered from knowledge rather than assumption? Have I kept reports within their actual scope? Does a record contradict anything I wrote? Is a lead-related process applicable, and have I kept it distinct from a general disclosure? What questions remain open?

  • Use plain, specific wording and avoid unsupported assurances.
  • Keep original records intact and identify their dates and scope.
  • Flag uncertainty and ask for guidance on unclear questions.
  • Update the transaction team if new relevant information comes to light.

Careful preparation is not about producing the most expansive statement possible. It is about making the information you have organized, accurate, and understandable, while acknowledging its limits. Use the current documents and applicable guidance for your transaction, and let qualified professionals address questions that require legal or technical interpretation.

Keep radon paperwork in the transaction file

CDPHE identifies its Radon and real estate transactions in Colorado brochure as a required brochure for real estate transactions and rental agreements. Bring that resource into your disclosure discussion and gather any prior test results, mitigation information and service records already available to you. Ask your broker or attorney how the current documents and requirements apply to your transaction; a testing plan is not a substitute for reviewing the paperwork.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Colorado seller disclosure form prove what every seller must disclose?+

No. The Colorado Division of Real Estate’s forms resource confirms that a Seller’s Property Disclosure form exists, but that fact alone does not establish a specific disclosure mandate, exemption, or answer for every transaction. Ask your broker or attorney which current form and requirements apply to your circumstances. For preparation, organize what you know and the records you have, and avoid treating general guidance as a complete statement of law.

How should I answer a disclosure question if I am unsure?+

Do not fill an information gap with a guess. Note what you personally know, what a record says, and what remains unconfirmed. If the wording is unclear or you need a technical interpretation, flag the question and seek an explanation from the appropriate transaction professional or qualified specialist. Preserve documents in their original scope, and do not turn a limited report or past repair record into a broader assurance about the property.

What is different about lead-based paint disclosure?+

The EPA says federal lead-based paint disclosure rules apply to most housing built before 1978, subject to exemptions. The process includes required materials and information before buyers sign, with an inspection opportunity subject to the rule and written arrangements. It is not a test and does not guarantee a property is lead-free. Check current EPA guidance for applicable exceptions and timing rather than relying on an assumed deadline.
